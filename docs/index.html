The Pitch

The problem with newborn paperwork is not that it is hard. It is that nobody tells you what applies to you, and the two items with real deadlines look exactly like the eighteen that do not.

Generic checklists make this worse. They list a passport next to a health plan enrollment window as though the two are comparable, and they cannot tell you which vital records office issues your child's birth certificate, because they do not know where you are.

Baby Docs asks a short set of questions, and every answer changes the list. Married or not. Job-based plan or Marketplace. Born in one state, living in another. Then it gives you the tasks that apply, ordered by when they close, each one with the documents you will be asked for and a link to the office that handles it.

It does not file anything. Filing a parentage form, an IRS election or an insurance enrollment on someone's behalf is a promise no app should make. It takes you to the official page and gets out of the way.

Baby Docs Plus

Every task, every deadline, every document checklist and every official link is free, for one child, on one device. Nothing with a date on it is ever behind a paywall: a deadline you cannot see is a deadline the app caused you to miss.

Plus is the work around the deadlines: a place to keep photographs of the documents themselves, tracking for the things you have sent and are waiting on, the packet your employer asks for, the printable one-pager, and further children. It is priced weekly, which is unusual and deliberate. The need is intense for six to thirteen weeks and then genuinely over, so a weekly price is the honest one for something that ends. There is a one-time option for people who want to keep the document vault for good.

What Mattered

Only two deadlines are hard. The 30-day job-based enrollment window and the 60-day Marketplace one are the only things the app will send a notification about. Everything else is a suggestion and is drawn as one. An app that treats a passport like an insurance window teaches you to ignore both.

Every rule cites its source. Each task carries the government URL its rule came from and the date someone last read that page, on the task itself rather than behind an info button. A rules app whose rules quietly go stale is worse than no app.

A verified state beats fifty guessed ones. Where we have read a state's own vital records page, the task links straight there and says so. Everywhere else it links to the federal directory, which has a state picker and is maintained by someone else. General and correct beats specific and wrong.

Changing an answer never destroys your work. Move from a job-based plan to the Marketplace and the plan rebuilds, but a task you have already completed, assigned or gathered documents for stays exactly as you left it.
