Newborn paperwork checklist: birth certificate, Social Security number and health insurance

By Jack Wallner · Updated October 12, 2026

Three things come first after a birth in the United States: the birth certificate, the Social Security number and card, and adding the baby to a health plan. The hospital starts the first two on the birth registration form, and the Social Security card usually arrives within about four weeks. The health plan is the one with a hard deadline: at least 30 days from the birth for a job-based plan, and 60 days for a Marketplace plan.

1. Birth certificate

Birth certificates are issued by the vital records office of the state or territory where the baby was born, not by the hospital and not by the federal government. The hospital collects the details, usually on a birth registration worksheet, and files the record with the state. The CDC keeps a directory of every state and territory office, and USAGov points to that directory when you need a certified copy.

Fees, forms, ID rules and turnaround differ by state, and the federal pages do not list them. Ask the issuing office three things: how long until the record is filed, how many certified copies you are likely to need, and whether you can order online, by mail or in person. When the copy arrives, read the name, date and parent details against what you intended. Fixing a mistake is a state process.

2. Social Security number and card

Most parents never fill out a Social Security application. Through the Enumeration at Birth program, you can request a number for the baby during hospital birth registration. The state's vital statistics bureau sends the registration data to the Social Security Administration (SSA), which assigns the number, issues the card and records the proof of birth.

SSA says the national average processing time for these cases is 2 weeks, and parents may wait up to an additional 2 weeks for the card in the mail. So plan on roughly two to four weeks from registration. If you were not offered the option, or you are not sure the request was made, ask the hospital's birth registrar or contact SSA instead of waiting indefinitely.

SSA has also announced a commemorative card with a Freedom 250 logo for children born in the United States between July 2 and December 31, 2026, issued through Enumeration at Birth. It works the same as a standard card.

You need the number for the tax return and for several accounts, so it is worth confirming early. Do not type the number into notes, emails or apps that are not built to hold it.

3. Health insurance

This is the paperwork with a clock on it, and which clock depends on how you are covered.

Job-based plan: at least 30 days

The Department of Labor says that when a birth happens, you must request special enrollment in an employer plan within 30 days of the birth. Coverage for special enrollees is effective retroactive to the date of birth. Your plan may allow longer, so ask your benefits administrator for the exact date and work to whichever is sooner. Federal special enrollment rules set a minimum, and the plan's summary plan description spells out how to request it.

Marketplace plan: 60 days

HealthCare.gov lists having a baby as a qualifying life event. You can enroll for up to 60 days afterward, and coverage can start on the day of the birth even when you enroll later in that window. If your state runs its own marketplace, the same 60-day window applies but you enroll on your state's site.

Medicaid and CHIP: any time

You can apply for Medicaid and CHIP any time of year, so there is no enrollment window to miss. Eligibility and the agency are set by your state.

A worked example

Baby born October 12. A job-based plan wants the request by November 11, which is 30 days later. A Marketplace plan allows until December 11, which is 60 days later. If you are unsure which applies, treat November 11 as your date until the benefits administrator tells you otherwise.

What else usually follows

Once those three are moving, the rest is mostly timing: claiming parental leave, updating your W-4, choosing a dependent care account if your employer offers one, claiming the baby as a tax dependent, a passport if you plan to travel, and a college savings or newborn account. These depend on your employer, your state and your tax situation, so they are best checked against your own paperwork rather than a general list.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get a newborn's Social Security card?

SSA says the national average processing time for Enumeration at Birth cases is 2 weeks, and parents may expect to wait up to an additional 2 weeks for the card in the mail. State timing varies, so ask the hospital registrar if it has not arrived after about a month.

How long do I have to add a newborn to my health insurance?

For a job-based plan, you must request special enrollment within 30 days of the birth, and your plan may allow longer. For a Marketplace plan the window is 60 days from the birth. Medicaid and CHIP accept applications any time of year.

Is the baby covered from the day of birth if I enroll later?

For a job-based plan, the Department of Labor says special enrollment coverage is effective retroactive to the date of birth. HealthCare.gov says Marketplace coverage can start the day of the event even if you enroll up to 60 days afterward.

Who issues a newborn's birth certificate?

The vital records office of the state or territory where the baby was born issues it, not the hospital. The hospital files the birth registration with the state, and you then order certified copies from that office.

Do I have to apply for the Social Security number myself?

Usually not. At most hospitals you can request the number on the birth registration form through the Enumeration at Birth program, and SSA mails the card. If you did not request it there, contact SSA to apply.
